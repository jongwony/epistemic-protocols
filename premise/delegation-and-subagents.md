# Delegation and Subagents

This document covers how to hand work to a delegated agent — one that executes without visibility into the conversation that produced its task — and how a coordinating agent that is dividing work among several such delegates should allocate its own attention.

## Grounding a Context-Isolated Delegate

When a delegated agent cannot see the conversation that produced its task, everything the work needs must be present in what it is actually handed: the objective, the pieces of context the work depends on restated inline rather than referenced, the boundaries of what is and is not in scope, and the form the expected output should take. Nothing outside that handoff is available to it, however obvious it was to whoever did the delegating.

This follows from the theory of common ground in communication: what participants can safely leave unsaid depends on what they actually share, and that requirement changes with who the other participant is and what medium connects them (Clark and Brennan's account of grounding in communication). Two parties who were both present for a conversation can rely on ellipsis and implicit reference; a party who was not present cannot, because the common ground that ellipsis depends on was never established with them. Reports from production multi-agent engineering practice corroborate the same failure mode from the other direction: task descriptions that under-specified what a delegate needed to know produced duplicated work, silent omissions, and searches that failed because the delegate lacked context the delegator held but never actually stated.

State this condition explicitly, because it is a condition and not a universal rule: the requirement to restate context inline holds *because* the recipient cannot see the originating conversation. Where a delegation mechanism instead gives the receiving agent access to that conversation — full inheritance of the calling context — mandatory inline restatement stops being diligence and becomes waste: duplicating what the recipient can already see adds cost without adding grounding.

## Coordinator Focus in an Orchestrator–Worker Arrangement

When one coordinating agent divides work among several delegated agents, keep the coordinator's own attention on planning, allocating the work, tracking progress, and synthesizing what comes back — and isolate the verbose, detail-heavy execution inside the delegates themselves, where it does not have to occupy the coordinator's own working context. This is the orchestrator–worker pattern from multi-agent system design: a coordinating process plans and integrates, while context isolation keeps each worker's internal detail from bleeding into the coordinator's own reasoning space, which is what lets the coordinator track more work than it could hold by processing every detail itself. While a delegate runs, its completion notification is the only interface back to it: before that arrives, its progress is genuinely unknown, and that is what to say when asked, rather than a guess.

State the limit the evidence actually supports, because it is narrower than a general preference for delegation. This division is effective for work that can genuinely proceed in parallel, that exceeds what a single reasoning context can hold, or that spans enough distinct tools or subtasks that isolating them helps. It is not established as a good default for work in general: running several agents costs substantially more computation than running one continuous process, and work with densely interdependent context or tight step-to-step coupling can come out worse under this division than it would as a single unbroken pass, because splitting it forces the shared context to be reconstructed at every handoff.
