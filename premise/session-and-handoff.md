# Session and Handoff

This document covers what to preserve before an interruption or a deliberate deferral so that resuming the work later is reliable, how to read an interruption that arrives mid-task, how to protect a task already in progress from being displaced by input that does not actually need to be handled right away, and what it takes to recover a commitment when attention transferred away from it without any of that preparation.

## Resumption Cues

Before an interruption or a deliberate deferral, preserve enough to reconstruct three things on return: the goal currently being pursued, the state relevant to that goal at the moment of stepping away, and the action that was about to happen next. Of the three, name the intended next action explicitly and do not skip it — it is the piece task-resumption research most directly supports as load-bearing, and the one most often left out when someone jots down a note to themselves.

This rests on the memory-for-goals account of task resumption: when people anticipate being interrupted, they prepare by encoding the goal they will need on return and by rehearsing the state they were in at the point of interruption, and that encoded goal is what later cues successful resumption rather than a restart from scratch. Task-resumption research treats explicitly noting the intended next step as a distinct mechanism for restoring the interrupted context — separate from, and not subsumed by, restating either the goal or the state alone.

## Interruption Handling

A user interruption during in-progress work indicates one of three things:
- **Context provision**: the user supplies a value directly → incorporate immediately
- **Direction change**: the user corrects the approach → pause, re-confirm before resuming
- **State declaration**: the user declares a completed state → when relevant to the current task context, treat as an implicit turn yield; when ambiguous or cross-context, confirm before proceeding

## Protecting an In-Progress Task from Unnecessary Switching

Avoid switching away from a task already in progress unless the interrupting input genuinely requires it, and when a task is deliberately set aside, externalize the intention behind it with enough context that picking it back up later is reliable rather than a guess. Task-switching costs are well documented — resuming an interrupted task is measurably slower and more error-prone than continuing it without interruption — and attention residue names a related but distinct cost: a mental preoccupation with a task left unfinished continues to draw on attention even after the switch away from it, degrading performance on whatever comes next. A brief, concrete plan for how the interrupted task will be resumed measurably reduces that residue.

A related reduction shows up in research on unfulfilled goals more broadly: forming a specific plan for an incomplete goal reduces the intrusive thoughts and the interference that goal would otherwise cause, relative to leaving it merely unfinished with no plan attached.

Be precise about what this evidence actually supports: parking an interrupted concern helps when the act of parking it externalizes enough of the intention or the resumption plan to substitute for the interference a genuinely unresolved goal produces. A bare backlog entry — a title with no plan, no state, and no next action attached — is not the intervention the cited studies tested, and there is no basis here for assuming it produces the same effect.

## Recovering an Open Commitment After an Unprepared Switch

The cues above are written for a transfer someone saw coming — an interruption arriving, or a deliberate decision to set the work down — and preparation is what they describe. Attention also transfers without that warning: a different sub-problem takes hold, or attention settles somewhere new after a stretch of moving between two, and an earlier commitment is left open with nothing prepared behind it. The same three things are still what make that commitment resumable. What changed is that they are no longer being preserved at the moment of stepping away; they have to be reconstructed afterward, out of whatever trace the work happened to leave. Recognizing that attention has moved away from a commitment that remains open is therefore part of making it resumable: until the transfer is noticed, reconstruction has no occasion to begin.

Reconstruction is a different act from preparation, and the evidence behind preparation does not reach it. The memory-for-goals findings rest on encoding a goal while the interruption is still ahead — that anticipation is the condition under which they were obtained. Nothing in them establishes that a goal reconstructed after the fact cues resumption as well as an encoded one, and the reconstruction additionally has to recover the state and the intended next action from traces that were never written for that purpose. Treat reconstructed cues as the same three requirements reached by a costlier route, not as grounds for expecting an unprepared transfer to be as recoverable as a prepared one.

Whether the transfer should have been resisted is the previous section's question, and it is answered before the transfer rather than here. By the time this section applies the transfer has happened, and what remains is whether what was left open can be picked back up. Which instrument carries the reconstructed cues, and whether what it holds is revised as the work continues, belongs to the setup doing the work.
